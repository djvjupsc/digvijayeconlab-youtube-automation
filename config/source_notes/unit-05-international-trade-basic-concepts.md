# Source notes — International Trade: Basic Concepts and Analytical Tools

These notes support introductory trade theory. They do not establish official MPPSC syllabus alignment.

## Absolute and comparative advantage

OpenStax, *Principles of Economics 2e*, section 33.1, explains absolute advantage in terms of producing more output with the same resources (or using fewer resources per unit), while comparative advantage is determined by lower opportunity cost. A country may have absolute advantage in both goods and still gain from specialization and trade because relative opportunity costs differ.

Source: https://openstax.org/books/principles-of-economics-2e/pages/33-1-absolute-and-comparative-advantage

OpenStax defines comparative advantage as producing a good at a lower cost in terms of other goods, i.e. a lower opportunity cost.
Source: https://openstax.org/books/principles-of-economics-2e/pages/2-key-terms

## Ricardo's original publication date

Cambridge University Press identifies David Ricardo's *On the Principles of Political Economy, and Taxation* as originally published in 1817. The Library of Economics and Liberty edition separately identifies the first publication date as 1817 and notes that the online text is its 1821 third edition. Therefore, write "first published in 1817"; do not imply that every edition is dated 1817.

Sources:
- Cambridge University Press: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/on-the-principles-of-political-economy-and-taxation/5C17BF2152379956950601EFE05AE14F
- Library of Economics and Liberty (edition note): https://www.econlib.org/library/Ricardo/ricP.html

## Model scope and production possibilities

- The simple Ricardian model is a production-side account of comparative advantage, based on relative unit-labour requirements / relative opportunity costs. Hicks–Slutsky income and substitution effects belong to consumer-demand analysis and must not be inserted as an explanation of Ricardian comparative advantage.
- In the simplest constant-unit-labour-requirement Ricardian setup, the production-possibility frontier is linear and opportunity cost is constant. A generally concave PPF represents increasing marginal opportunity cost; do not conflate this general case with the simple constant-cost Ricardian assumption.
- When a table gives output per worker/hour, compute opportunity costs from the output possibilities. When it gives labour required per unit, compute the opportunity cost from the labour requirements. Do not mix these representations.
- Label countries, goods, units and time/resource constraints. All numerical examples not taken from a cited data source must be explicitly hypothetical.


## Explicit simplification used in the lesson example

For the numerical two-country, two-good teaching example, state explicitly that transport costs are assumed to be zero **for model simplicity**. This is a simplifying assumption of that example, not a claim that real international trade has no freight, insurance, border or other trade costs. Do not leave this as an unresolved verification claim; retain the caveat that actual trade costs can be positive.
