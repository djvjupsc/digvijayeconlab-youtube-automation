# Source notes — Market Failure and Remedial Measures

These notes support the opening Unit 6 topic: asymmetric information, public goods and externalities. They do not establish official MPPSC syllabus alignment.

## Public goods

OpenStax, *Principles of Economics 2e*, section 13.3, defines a pure public good by two properties: non-excludability and non-rivalry. Non-excludability means it is costly or impossible to prevent non-payers from using it; non-rivalry means one person's use does not reduce availability to others. The free-rider problem can weaken private incentives to provide such goods. A good does not become a pure public good merely because government pays for it.

Source: https://openstax.org/books/principles-of-economics-2e/pages/13-3-public-goods

## Imperfect and asymmetric information

OpenStax, *Principles of Economics 2e*, section 16.1, distinguishes imperfect information (one or more parties lack information needed to make a fully informed decision) from asymmetric information (one party has more relevant information than the other). Adverse selection and moral hazard are distinct mechanisms; define them separately and give a context-appropriate example rather than treating the terms as synonyms.

Source: https://openstax.org/books/principles-of-economics-2e/pages/16-1-the-problem-of-imperfect-information-and-asymmetric-information

## Externalities and social costs

OpenStax, *Principles of Economics 2e*, section 12.1, discusses pollution as a negative externality and distinguishes private from social costs. An externality affects a third party outside the market transaction. For a negative production externality, marginal social cost (MSC) exceeds marginal private cost (MPC) by the marginal external cost, under the stated model assumptions.

Source: https://openstax.org/books/principles-of-economics-2e/pages/12-1-the-economics-of-pollution

## Adverse selection — original source

George A. Akerlof, “The Market for ‘Lemons’: Quality Uncertainty and the Market Mechanism,” *The Quarterly Journal of Economics*, Vol. 84, No. 3 (August 1970), pp. 488–500, DOI: 10.2307/1879431. The article uses used automobiles to illustrate how quality uncertainty and asymmetric information can affect market outcomes.

Source: https://doi.org/10.2307/1879431

## Editorial safeguards

- Do not equate every publicly funded service with a pure public good.
- Distinguish non-rivalry from non-excludability.
- Distinguish adverse selection (hidden information/characteristics before a contract or transaction) from moral hazard (hidden action or changed incentives after an agreement), and qualify examples.
- Identify whether an externality is from production or consumption and whether it is positive or negative.
- For Pigouvian tax diagrams, state the model conditions and relate the tax to marginal external damage at the efficient quantity; do not imply every real-world tax is exactly optimal.
- Label numerical examples as hypothetical unless an official data source is supplied.
